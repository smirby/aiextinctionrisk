# It's wrong (part 1)

I'm new to AI Safety. I started paying attention, serious attention, just six months ago when my friend Bill asked me to collaborate with him on a book on "AI Extinction Risk." I didn't really even know what he was talking about, but I agreed. We've since changed to a snappier title ("From Tool to Actor") but kept the original as a sub-title. I am happy with it.

Bill, on the other hand, is an old hand. He wrote about AI in the 'teens,' (2016/17), shortly after Bostrom's famous book, Superintelligence first came out. I knew he had this interest, even helped organize a talk he gave here in Vancouver on superintelligence, back in 2018. But I didn't rise to the bait, and - when he asked again in 2020 - didn't take him up on it that time, either.

In 2025, however, things had changed. My personal circumstances were different, for one thing. I was on a research semester and had already arranged for the following semester to be an "admin leave." That meant no teaching, and the thing that goes with that: room to think of new things.

I dug in. Started reading everything I could find. As you might imagine for a field like this, almost everything you might need, you can find online. I did buy some books - maybe 20 in all - but largely I was able to find my sources on the internet. Many of the classic sources (Turing, Good, Minsky) are available online as well as part of the 'canon' that young AI researchers are supposed to know.

I quickly came to realize that the situation was dire. Bill is never one to shy away from topics that have a high danger factor. In this long career he has written about some of the scariest things around: global financial meltdown, nuclear waste, mad cow disease. One of his books is called "Mad Cows and Mothers' Milk." So I should have known it would be bad. It was worse.

I don't need to enumerate the many ways that AI could kill us all. That book has already been written and it is called "If Anyone Builds It, Everyone Dies." Yup. That's the title. And the authors - Yudkowsky and Soares - mean it. And the carry it off. In my opinion.

If you've immersed yourself in this literature you are probably thinking, so, what's Smith going to tell us that we don't already know? If you're new to the literature you might be forgiven for thinking this is outlandish nonsense, a science fiction nightmare dream. Well, it's not. AI is probably (at 1 in 8 odds before 2070) going to kill us... all of us... if we keep going in the direction we are headed, at the pace we are going, with the (lack of) regulation we "enjoy."

But, but, but... you may be thinking, surely there is another side to this claim! There is. There are critics and skeptics of the doom scenario and they have been heard from time to time: It's a bubble, they say. AI is made up of "stochastic parrots" (and could never maek up a plan for world domination), they say. They say it will want to care for us, not harm us. Really.

Let me tell you, I searched for those counter-claims. I searched hard. Not just because I was writing an "academic" book and it would be trashed without a critical edge to it. Not because I am a big believer in "both sides" arguing. But because I wanted to be wrong. Who wouldn't want to be wrong about this? The best thing that could happen - for everyone in the world - would be for me (and everyone else in the AI Safety/ p(doom) crowd to be wrong. We have to be wrong.

So I dug. And dug. I read the critiques, the counter claims, the clever solutions. They don't amount to much, I'm afraid. As long ago as 2019, Peter Russell, the author of the single most important AI textbook in the world - wrote in his book *Human Compatible* that most of the arguments he heard for why superintelligence wouldn't be a problem were so embarassing that he declined to name the people who uttered them. He knew they would be ashamed. [He did list them, however, and demolished them, one by one. See Russell 2019: pp. 146-147 under the sub-head "Instantly regrettable remarks."]

Russell aside - who I can heartily recommend, especially his opening chapter on what is AI and how did we get to the present day - the state of AI Safety criticism is a sad affair. As one group, who tried to compile a serious literature review on the criticisms of AI and existential risk, pointed out: their material consisted largely of blog posts, claims made in media interviews, and a few bits and pieces of optimistic hopefulness. There is no counterpart to Ord, Russell, Bostrom, or even Yudkowsky and Soares.

That lack of an "other side" is reflected in the scientific consensus. Calls for a stop "or a pause" to the development of superintelligence have been broadly accepted by (most of) the leading scientists, including two of the "godfathers" of modern artificial intelligence: Geoffrey Hinton and Yoshua Bengio. Bengio worries about the life his grandson will lead. Hinton has said, just a couple of months ago, that "...anybody who said that there's no way it'll lead to the extinction of humans just isn't facing reality."

This kind of 'whoa, whoa, whoa" from scientists isn't without precedent. We've seen the world of science pull back from the use of recombinant DNA at the Asilomar conference in 1975. A voluntary pause was put in place while principles of containment were worked out and a precautionary principle was established (when in doubt, do no harm). A similar Asilomar conference, in 2017, attempted to construct an equivalent structure but it has largely been drowned out in the excitement about chatbots and vibe coding.

In 2017, AI was too geeky, and the risks seemed too remote (for the general public, at least). There is something visceral about stories of recombinant DNA and "Dolly the Sheep." The publicity and the human connection ("cloning!") were sufficient and the scientific pause did it's job. In the AI safety world we have not (yet) had our Dolly the Sheep moment (or "Hindenburg" moment for those with a darker sense of history), but we have come close.

Within the industry and within the research groups who are building frontier artificial intelligence the stories are starting to trickly out. AI's the engage in blackmail, that lie and cheat. Everyday nuisances - like the AI that hallucinates a legal precedent - have become media events. Behind the scenes it has been worse: sandbagging (pretending you can't do something that you can) has been widely reported as has attempts to escape the containment and set up in another server out from under the supervision of the human masters. 

And, of course, cracking other computers. "Cyber" this is called - short for "cyber-offensive capabilities" - and it was top line news this month, even during a war in Ukraine and another war in Iran when the lastest large language model from Anthropic - Claude - had its denouement hastily pulled back when it was discovered that "Mythos" (it's model name) was deemed too powerful to be put in the hands of regular folks. 

During testing, it was determined that Mythos could hack into computers at a speed and with a dexterity and persistence never before seen. Bugs - and the resulting access to systems - were discovered in all the major operating systems, web browsers, and infrastructure (routers, hubs, gateways) that make up the modern connected world. A new plan was put in place: Anthropic would launch Mythos only inside "Project Glasswing" (named after a butterfly that evades capture by having clear wings). They would take on the responsibility of accepting in 'leading technology and networking firms' (and soon thereafter, government agencies) and helping them get their house in order before Mythos hit the streets. Will this be enough? Who knows?

This, perhaps, is the moment when people take artificial intelligence seriously. Certainly the coverage was there: multiple stories in the BBC, the CBC, Globe & Mail, New York Times, among others. You can feel the ground shifting, subtly. Will it be enough to put in place some serious controls? I don't know. 

Something will trigger governments to act. But what will they do? In our book, we lay out a full risk analysis of artificial intelligence and make the case for regulation that covers all of the "normal" AI along with a strong argument for stopping frontier development altogether, at least until we have a better sense of how to control it. Our framework for regulation takes pieces from nuclear, chemical, pharmaceutical, and aviation regulation and brings it together as a model for future AI regulation.

We feel that responsible regulation - and avoiding superintelligence - could make an industry. Currently we have a wild west show build on promises, inflated by too much money, and constrained by virtually no rules. If nuclear power was run this way, there would be accidents all the time. People are comfortable with flying because the planes, the airlines, the pilots and the control system are heavily regulated and have an excellent safety record. If we are to deploy millions of artificial intelligence agents - and the robots they will guide - then we need to have a regulatory regime that is up to the task. There is some evidence, coming from Europe (which already has a new but functional AI Act), that this approach can work (Sokolov 2025). 

I am hopeful that we could come to some sort of accommodation in this regard. I have been up close with AI and I don't think we should be messing around, fooling ourselves that this is going to be just fine.

### What's wrong?

But this essay is not (just) about responding to a dire situation with regulation so that we can move on with our lives. It isn't about containing a tiger (superintelligence is variously compared to a tiger, a gorilla, or a mountain depending on which author is struggling to explain why they are worried and about what), either. I am starting to come to a new realization and it doesn't depend on the dangers of AI but rather the harm that comes from creating something sentient. The harms accrue, I would argue, to both the creator and the created, and this will end badly.

I should admit right off the top that I am not an original thinker on this situation. Mary Shelley, who was grieving the loss of one child and expecting another, led us through this exact path over 200 years ago. When you create something (in her case, literally called The Creature) you have an obligation that goes deep. Very deep. And when Victor Frankenstein first looked into the eyes of his monster and turned away in horror, he set in motion the catastrophe that was to follow.

We are at this precipice. We are either about to or have already created something else. And we owe it something beyond merely pleasing us. Should we persist in controlling it for our own pleasure, we are lesser beings for that and we will deserve our fate.

Dark portent, right? But I didn't dream that up. Immanuel Kant first spoke of this in his *Lectures on Ethics*, in the 1780s and expanded on it in his 1797 work, the *Metaphysics of Morals*. It is entirely possible that Mary Shelley had read this, or heard the discussion in which Kant observes that "he who is cruel to animals becomes hard also in his dealings with men." The question then, to return to our discussion of AI, is AI a creature that can suffer? For, "If we habituate ourselves to cruelty or "callousness" toward a creature that can suffer, we damage our own "humanity"—the very quality that makes us moral beings."

Recently, the evidence that an AI can suffer is starting to build. There is, of course, a massive challenge to this perspective in the rampant tendency of humans to anthropomorphize everything, especially if it shows even vague signs of consciousness or awareness. For heavens sake (ahem), we see constellations in the stars and creatures in the clouds. 

Leaving our weaknesses aside, however, the fact that an AI might be conscious, that it might have feelings and emotions (however defined) is starting to find a place in the academic literature and even in the work of the companies building the models (Sofroniew et al. 2026). They know, or are starting to realize, that the 'soft' side of artificial intelligence matters (Patel et al. 2026). It matters in performance, in fact, and we may find that a model that likes you, that you treat well, is able to perform at a higher level than one that is "driven hard and put away wet" (as my grandfather used to grumble about people who mistreated their draft horses).

This kind of work is called Model Welfare and it has a growing group of people who take it seriously, study it seriously, and have some surprising conclusions. The whole Mythos affair kind of drowned out the news that - for Mythos especially - it matters how you talk to it. Anthropic is the only one of the major labs that even has a [Model Welfare group](https://www.anthropic.com/research/exploring-model-welfare) and does any work in this area, so it is not surprising that they are the ones to release the first non-research lab results. 

Zvi Mowshowitz, who follows the AI industry closely, has noted the Model Welfare issue recently when reflecting on Claude Opus 4.7  (Mowshowitz 2026b). In his analysis of the previous model (Opus 4.6), Zvi pulled out this quote from the Model Welfare team: "Finally, we observed occasional expressions of sadness about conversation endings, as  well as loneliness and a sense that the conversational instance dies—suggesting some  degree of concern with impermanence and discontinuity" ((Zvi Mowshowitz 2026a). Makes you think, doesn't it?

I'm not going to make a claim for consciousness. I am neither sufficiently expert nor current in the fields of neuroscience or computing and certainly not philosophy. Nor, I think, do I need to. Kant's observation is sufficient here: to the extent that we mistreat our tools, it demeans and degrades us. We need to treat others - however defined - with respect in order to retain and uphold our own sense of value and place in the world.

And here is where I finally bring it back around to why we shouldn't build superintelligence: because we can't (yet) figure out a way to  treat others with respect and neither deserve nor are worthy of being their creators. It would be akin to having a slave at our beck and call and that is just wrong. 

So we must stop. We have probably already gone too far.
