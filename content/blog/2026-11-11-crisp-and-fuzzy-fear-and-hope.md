Let me sketch an argument that has emerged of late. It builds on some work that dates to 2024, and started with Jan Leike's (2024) post on "Crisp and Fuzzy Tasks." In that post he notes that some things are verifiable and thus it is easy (easier?) to train an AI model to solve them with reinforcement learning since you can tell the model when it is correct, and reward it. Mathematics, computer programming, physics, all fall into this category. He calls these "crisp" tasks. 

Other things are not so clearcut and don't necessarily have a right answer or the answer depends on circumstances or context. Think of legal questions, moral questions, ethical questions. These are not straightforwardly right or wrong and thus not readily verifiable. Reinforcement learning takes longer and therefor progress in these domains is slower. Leike calls these fuzzy tasks.

> Crisp tasks are more reasoning/system-2 based. Whether a response is good is typically precisely defined. Reasonable, knowledgeable people don’t disagree about it.
>
> Examples: math, coding competitions, physics questions, logic puzzles, basic factual knowledge, …
>
> Fuzzy tasks are more intuition/system-1 based. Whether a response is good is typically somewhat vaguely defined or could fall within a range. Reasonable, knowledgeable people may disagree about it.
>
> Examples: distinguishing cats and dogs in pictures, recognizing strong go moves, poetry writing, assistant helpfulness ratings, …

Leike points out that alignment research - and therefore advances in AI alignment - has a lot of these fuzzy tasks.

A few authors, including Markus Bowkis et al. (2026) and Timothy Lee (2026) have confirmed that advances on fuzzy tasks proceed at a slower rate than "crisp" tasks. This is sometimes called "jagged" progress: the performance jumps up on some things, and drops on others. The phenomenon is particularly noticeable in the world of robotics, where the device is constantly in contact with an unpredictable and varying environment, and the lessons from that world can help AI researchers with these challenges (Lee 2026).

But does jagged progress matter? Some writers have noticed differential progress and wonder if the slower pace on fuzzy tasks might mean that - for some things - advances in AI will not be as fast as otherwise predicted, and that humans will continue to be necessary. In other words, we need humans for the fuzzy side of life. That’s the hope of Arvind Narayanan (2025) who speaks of AI as "normal technology." 

The claim that fuzziness slows things down is elaborated by Nathan Lambert (2026), who notes the slower progress on fuzzy tasks and suggests this may delay or forestall "real" superintelligence. Alec Harris (2026) makes a similar same point, even claiming a chance of *deceleration* in AI progress, while admitting that there is still a significant chance of ASI by the end of 2027. Is this a hopeful sign and a potential counteraction to [AI takeover](https://treacheroustech.ca/blog/ai-takeover-etymology/)?

I am not so sure. The fear I have is that AI, being better at crisp, might just focus on crisp tasks - and even seek to run the world (in a takeover scenario) just as a bunch of "crisp problems," leaving our ethics, law, morality, and politics in the dustbin. Rather than seeing lower capabilities in fuzzy tasks as the problem, AI might instead see the fuzzy side of life *itself* as the problem. And then, to solve *that* problem you simply need to remove the fuzzy (compassion, forgiveness, morality, ethics) from the world (“if all you have is a hammer, everything looks like a nail”) and create a system without all those fuzzy aspects, and therefore without the need for humans.

And humans, being unnecessary, are dispensable.
