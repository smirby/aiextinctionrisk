When I was a teenager, growing up on a farm in SW Manitoba, I joined a club called "4-H". 4-H is a children's club, not unlike Cubs or Scouts, but with a farm-focused approach. And fewer badges. The four H's stood for head, heart, hands, and health. It has a long history and continues to this day. https://4-hontario.ca/about-4-h/history/ For our purposes, it is a mnemonic for four H's that hang over artificial intelligence like a message from hell: Holocaust, Heart of darkness, Hollow men, and now, Hiroshima (Ash 2026).

At the outset, I need to clarify that we are not equating AI to the Holocaust. The term is too serious for "equation." What we ARE doing, however, is looking at the Holocaust (as we do in Chapter 12 of [our book]({{< relref "../" >}}) for insight into the apparent ability of ordinary people to participate in unspeakable crimes, namely the creation of a superintelligence with the probable potential to exterminate humans.

## Holocaust

When we wrote about the Holocaust in our book about AI and existential risk, it was in part to bring to mind the aspect of that era that revealed the banality of evil and how people could get involved in a vast bureaucracy of destruction simply by going along with what others were doing. Tragically, the vortex was shameful and harmful and yet persisted.

## Heart of Darkness

We also explore, in Chapter 12, the implications of Joseph Conrad's Heart of Darkness and the pitiless pursuit of a petty project - making as much money as possible - was perverted into something that was unrecognizable, insupportable, and singularly evil.

## Hollow Men

Both the Holocaust and the Heart of Darkness reveal to us the depths of depravity possible by those who lose (or never developed) a moral compass. The notion that the CEOs of frontier AI companies will - with no apparent compunction - lead the entire human race off a cliff suggests to us that we need to reconsider the presence of hollow men amongst us once more.

## Hiroshima

In Timoth Garton Ash's 22 August opinion piece for *The Guardian,* we encounter the discouraging prophecy that it will take not just one, but multiple catastrophes to make people come to their senses about the dangers of artificial intelligence. Comparing AI to nuclear weapons, he observes that it is as if there are 10 simultaneous Manhattan Projects all running at once and all of them fiercely competitive, and "[u]nlike with nuclear weapons, there’s also no obvious logic of “mutually assured destruction” to tame the fierce geopolitical competition" (Ash 2026). When we have a disaster, he notes, it won't affect the whole world equally. And so, 

> It therefore seems unlikely that just one disaster will be enough to bring us humans to our senses, acting together to control the superhuman power we are creating. I really hate to say this, but … an AI Hiroshima? We should be so lucky. (Ash 2026)

The last H. Hiroshima is now in the realm of discussion when we are talking about AI. When it comes to "what it is reasonable to talk about," there is a concept for that in public policy: the [Overton Window](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Overton_window). An idea moves from unthinkable to radical to acceptable to sensible to popular to policy. With an opinion piece in The Guardian, we have moved the idea that AI could potentially kill millions of people (perhaps everyone) closer to the "policy" category, and further away from the unthinkable.
